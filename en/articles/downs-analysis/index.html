Downs Cephalometric Analysis: Landmarks, Measurements and Norms

November 16, 2026 · 9 min read

The Downs analysis is one of the classic cephalometric methods. William B. Downs described it in 1948 in his paper “Variations in facial relationships: their significance in treatment and prognosis” and later expanded it in his work on the analysis of the dentofacial profile (1956). Several measurements that are now part of other analyses — the facial angle, the angle of convexity, the Y-axis — were first brought together into one system by Downs. This article covers the landmarks and planes of the analysis, its ten measurements, their norms, and why these norms cannot be applied to every patient without adjustment.

The basis of the analysis: the Frankfort horizontal

Downs relates almost all measurements to the Frankfort horizontal (FH), the line from porion (Po, the top of the external auditory meatus) to orbitale (Or, the lowest point of the orbital margin). This distinguishes it from the Steiner analysis, which uses the S–N line as its reference. The second key element is the facial plane N–Pog: the line from nasion (N, the most anterior point of the frontonasal suture) to pogonion (Pog, the most anterior point of the chin).

The measurements fall into two groups of five: skeletal measurements describe the position of the jaws and the direction of growth, and dental measurements describe the position of the incisors and the occlusal plane relative to the skeleton.

Landmarks and lines

  • N — nasion, the most anterior point of the frontonasal suture.
  • S — sella, the centre of the sella turcica.
  • Po and Or — porion and orbitale; FH passes through them.
  • A — the deepest point on the anterior contour of the maxilla between ANS and the alveolar process.
  • B — the deepest point on the anterior contour of the mandible between the alveolar process and the chin.
  • Pog — the most anterior point of the chin; Gn — gnathion; Me — menton, the lowest point of the symphysis; Go — gonion.
  • Incisal edges and root apices of the central incisors — for the incisor axes; the occlusal plane runs through the region of incisor overlap and first molar occlusion.

In current studies using the Downs analysis, the mandibular plane is usually drawn through gonion and menton (as, for example, in Puspitasari and Siregar, 2024). Because different schools construct this plane in different ways, it is useful to state in the report which construction was used.

Skeletal measurements

Facial angle

The angle between the Frankfort horizontal and the facial plane N–Pog. It shows the anteroposterior position of the chin: the smaller the angle, the further back the chin lies relative to FH; the larger, the more prominent it is.

Angle of convexity

The angle between the lines N–A and A–Pog. It describes the convexity of the skeletal profile: positive values mean that point A lies in front of the facial plane (a convex profile), negative values indicate a concave profile. The Downs mean is 0°.

A-B plane to facial plane

The angle between the A–B line and the facial plane N–Pog. It reflects the relationship of the apical bases of the two jaws relative to the profile. The normal value is negative: point B lies behind point A.

Mandibular plane angle

The angle between the mandibular plane and FH. High values are associated with a vertical growth pattern, low values with a horizontal one.

Y-axis

The angle between the S–Gn line and the Frankfort horizontal. It describes the direction of chin growth: as the angle increases, the chin moves downward and backward; as it decreases, the chin grows more forward.

Dental measurements

Cant of the occlusal plane

The angle between the occlusal plane and FH. A steeper occlusal plane usually accompanies a vertical facial pattern.

Interincisal angle

The angle between the long axes of the upper and lower central incisors. The smaller the angle, the more the incisors are tipped forward (proclined).

Lower incisor to occlusal plane

The angle between the long axis of the lower incisor and the occlusal plane. Downs recorded it as a deviation from a right angle, so the norm is a small number (14.5°) rather than an angle of about 104°.

Lower incisor to mandibular plane

The angle between the lower incisor axis and the mandibular plane, also recorded as a deviation from 90°. The norm of 1.4° corresponds to the familiar IMPA notation of about 91.4°. A larger value means more proclined lower incisors.

Upper incisor to the A–Pog line

The distance in millimetres from the incisal edge of the upper central incisor to the A–Pog line. The greater the distance, the more protrusive the upper incisors.

Why Downs norms cannot be applied to everyone

The Downs norms were obtained from a small sample of white Americans in the mid-20th century. Studies of other groups show clear differences, especially in incisor position.

In Rizvi's sample (91 people with well-balanced faces), the dental measurements differed significantly from the Downs norms in every parameter, and the author concludes that a single standard of facial aesthetics should not be applied to all ethnic groups. The Jakarta sample also shows more pronounced incisor protrusion and a larger mandibular plane angle. A value “outside the Downs norm” therefore does not by itself indicate pathology: it should be compared with norms obtained for the patient’s own group and age and assessed together with the clinical picture.

How to read the result

  • Start with the skeletal part: the facial angle and the angle of convexity show where the chin is and how convex the profile is; the A–B plane refines the relationship between the jaws.
  • Then the vertical: the mandibular plane angle and the Y-axis together indicate the direction of growth.
  • Only then the dental part: the interincisal angle, the lower incisor inclination and the position of the upper incisor relative to A–Pog show how the teeth compensate for or accentuate the skeletal pattern.
  • A single value outside the range matters less than a consistent picture formed by several measurements.

Where errors are most common

  • Porion and orbitale. Both structures are bilateral, and on a lateral radiograph their outlines often appear double. Four measurements of the analysis depend directly on how FH is drawn: the facial angle, the mandibular plane angle, the Y-axis and the cant of the occlusal plane. When outlines are doubled, the point is usually placed midway between them, and this is done the same way on all of the patient’s radiographs.
  • Incisor root apices. The long axis of an incisor runs through the incisal edge and the root apex, and the apex is the hardest part to see. A small shift of this point noticeably changes the interincisal angle and both lower incisor angles.
  • The occlusal plane. Different analyses construct it differently (through the incisors and molars, or through the posterior teeth only), so a cant value from another method cannot be compared directly with the Downs norm.
  • Millimetres. Angular measurements do not depend on radiographic magnification, but the distance from the upper incisor to A–Pog does. For this measurement the radiograph must be calibrated.

The Downs analysis combines well with other methods: for example, with the Steiner analysis, where the same jaws are assessed relative to S–N, or with the McNamara analysis, which relies more on linear measurements.
