Kim Cephalometric Analysis: APDI and ODI — Construction, Norms and Interpretation

November 23, 2026 · 8 min read

The Kim analysis consists of two angular indices proposed by orthodontist Young H. Kim: the ODI (Overbite Depth Indicator) and the APDI (Anteroposterior Dysplasia Indicator). The ODI was described in 1974 as a measure of the vertical skeletal tendency towards open bite or deep bite; the APDI followed in 1978, together with J. J. Vietas, as a measure of the sagittal jaw relationship. Each index combines several familiar planes into a single number, which makes them useful as a summary assessment on top of the classic measurements.

Landmarks and planes

Both indices use standard cephalometric references:

  • Frankfort horizontal (FH) — porion (Po) to orbitale (Or).
  • Palatal plane (PP) — ANS to PNS.
  • A-B plane — the line through points A and B.
  • Facial plane — nasion (N) to pogonion (Pog).
  • Mandibular plane (MP) — defined by Obamiyi et al. (2019) as the gonion–menton line.

Because FH is part of both indices, the result depends on how accurately porion and orbitale are located; Čelar et al. (2018) point to this as a source of error.

ODI: construction

The ODI is the angle between the A-B plane and the mandibular plane, to which the angle between the palatal plane and FH is added or from which it is subtracted:

ODI = ∠(A-B / MP) ± ∠(PP / FH)

The sign rule is the same in every source we reviewed (Yang et al. 1992; Shin and Chang 1996; Obamiyi et al. 2019; Caballero-Purizaga et al. 2019): when the palatal plane slopes upward anteriorly (ANS higher relative to FH), the PP–FH angle is read as negative and is subtracted; when it slopes downward anteriorly, the angle is added.

Meaning: the lower the ODI, the stronger the skeletal open-bite tendency; the higher the ODI, the stronger the deep-bite tendency. Beltrão (2015) gives a reference point: a value of 68° or less indicates a skeletal open-bite tendency.

APDI: construction

The APDI is the sum of three angles: the facial angle (FH to N-Pog), the A-B plane angle (A-B to N-Pog, after Downs) and the palatal plane angle to FH:

APDI = ∠(FH / N-Pog) ± ∠(A-B / N-Pog) ± ∠(PP / FH)

Signs: the A-B plane angle is negative when point B lies behind point A; the PP–FH angle is negative when the palatal plane slopes upward anteriorly and positive when it slopes downward (Yang et al. 1992; Shin and Chang 1996). Geometrically, the APDI equals the angle between the A-B plane and the palatal plane — this is how Obamiyi et al. (2019) and Caballero-Purizaga et al. (2019) describe it, and Čelar et al. (2018) measured it directly as the angle between PP and the AB line.

Interpretation: lower APDI values indicate a Class II tendency, higher values a Class III tendency. In the study by Kim and Vietas (1978), the APDI showed the highest correlation with molar displacement measured on casts (r = 0.643) and, unlike ANB, the A-B plane angle and the Wits appraisal, showed little overlap between distoclusion and mesioclusion.

Worked example

A hypothetical example to show how the sign rule works. Suppose the angle between the A-B plane and the mandibular plane is 72°, and the palatal plane slopes upward anteriorly by 2° relative to FH. The PP–FH angle is then −2°, and ODI = 72° − 2° = 70°. If the same plane sloped downward by 2°, the ODI would be 72° + 2° = 74°. The 4° difference comes from palatal inclination alone, so the sign of this component needs careful attention.

The APDI follows the same logic. Suppose the facial angle is 87°, point B lies behind point A with an A-B plane angle of −5°, and the palatal plane slopes upward by 1°. Then APDI = 87° − 5° − 1° = 81°. The numbers in this example are illustrative and do not describe any patient.

The norm values follow Obamiyi et al. (2019) and Hsu et al. (2024); the sample sizes follow the Kim and Vietas abstract in PubMed and Fatima et al. (2016). Some sources give rounded variants: ODI 74.5° ± 6.0° (Yang et al. 1992) and APDI 81.3° (Shin and Chang 1996). The original samples were white children with a mean age of about 11 years (according to Shin and Chang 1996: 10.8 years for the ODI and 11.5 years for the APDI).

For the APDI, Čelar et al. (2018) used the following cut-offs: retrognathic below 77.5°, neutral 77.5–85°, prognathic above 85°.

Combination factor and extraction index

Kim later proposed the combination factor CF = ODI + APDI. According to Kim (1979), as summarised by Shin and Chang (1996), the mean CF was 155.9° in Class II division 1 patients treated without extractions and 148.0° in those treated with extractions. Beltrão (2015) associates a CF above 155° with a low-angle pattern and a CF below 155° with a high-angle pattern, in which extraction is more likely.

Shin and Chang (1996) also describe Kim's extraction index (EI): (interincisal angle − 130°)/5 is added to the CF when the interincisal angle is above 130°, or (130° − interincisal angle)/5 is subtracted when it is below; the lip position relative to the E-line is then taken into account (the distance in millimetres is subtracted if the lip is in front of the line and added if it is behind). We could not find published EI cut-off values from Kim himself, so we do not give any.

Obamiyi et al. found that in African American children both indices were significantly lower than the white norms and increased with age, with no difference between sexes. The group a patient is compared with therefore matters for interpretation.

How reliable are the indices?

  • Freudenthaler et al. (2000), in 122 white patients, found that the APDI distinguished all three molar classes and matched the molar relationship in 88% of cases. The ODI separated deep bite from normal and open bite but did not separate normal from open bite; it matched the overbite in 81% of cases.
  • Fatima et al. (2016) reported an area under the ROC curve of 0.91 for the ODI when separating deep from open bite, but only 0.196 when separating normal from open bite. For the APDI the areas were 0.77 (Class I vs II), 0.74 (I vs III) and 0.88 (II vs III).
  • Han and Kim (1998), in a sample of 976 subjects, found the APDI to be the best of 15 compared measures for identifying both Class II and Class III.
  • The PP–FH component on its own contributed little to separating overbite groups (Yang et al. 1992; Fatima et al. 2016).
  • Dentoalveolar compensation can mask a skeletal discrepancy (Fatima et al. 2016), so the Kim indices are read together with incisor position and the clinical picture.
