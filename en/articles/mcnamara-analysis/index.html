McNamara Cephalometric Analysis: Landmarks, Measurements and Norms

November 13, 2026 · 9 min read

The McNamara analysis is one of the most widely used cephalometric analyses of the lateral ceph. James McNamara (University of Michigan) published it in 1984 in the American Journal of Orthodontics [1]. It is valued because most of its measurements are linear, in millimetres: it is easier to picture how many millimetres a jaw is displaced than how many degrees an angle has changed. Below are the landmarks, lines and norms of the analysis as given in the author's original paper, and data on how these norms differ between patient groups.

Where the analysis comes from

According to the author, the method is derived in part from the principles of the Ricketts and Harvold analyses, while the nasion perpendicular and the point A vertical are presumed to be original [1]. From Harvold comes the idea of comparing the "effective" lengths of the maxilla and mandible; from Ricketts, the facial axis and the Frankfort horizontal as the basis of the constructions.

McNamara's norms were derived from three samples [1]:

  • Bolton standards — longitudinal records of children, retraced and digitized;
  • Burlington — longitudinal data on children from the Burlington Orthodontic Research Centre (Canada);
  • Ann Arbor — 111 young adults with good to excellent faces, Class I occlusion and good skeletal balance; mean age 26 years 8 months for women and 30 years 9 months for men.

Where possible, the values include an 8% radiographic enlargement [1]. This matters: McNamara's linear norms should only be compared with a radiograph taken and calibrated under similar conditions.

Reference lines and landmarks

The Frankfort horizontal runs through anatomic porion (the superior aspect of the external auditory meatus) and orbitale (the lower border of the orbit) [1].

The nasion perpendicular is a line dropped from N perpendicular to the Frankfort horizontal [1]. The positions of point A and pogonion are measured from it.

The point A vertical is a line through point A parallel to the nasion perpendicular; the position of the upper incisor is measured from it [1].

Key landmarks [1]:

  • Co (condylion) — the most posterosuperior point on the outline of the mandibular condyle;
  • Gn (anatomic gnathion) — the most anteroinferior point of the symphysis; the facial axis uses a separate constructed gnathion, the intersection of the facial plane (N–Pog) and the mandibular plane;
  • A, Pog, ANS, Me — in their usual sense;
  • Ba and PTM (the posterosuperior point of the pterygomaxillary fissure) — for the facial axis.

In the McNamara analysis the mandibular plane is the Go–Me line [1].

The five parts of the analysis

1. Maxilla to cranial base

The main measurement is the distance from point A to the nasion perpendicular. The author gives 0 mm for the mixed dentition and 1 mm for adults (the step-by-step section says "0–1 mm") [1]. Point A in front of the line is positive, behind it negative. The author also reports the SNA angle: 82.4° ± 3.0° in women and 83.9° ± 3.2° in men in the Ann Arbor sample [1].

2. Mandible to maxilla

This is the most distinctive part of the analysis. Two lengths are measured:

  • effective midfacial length — Co–A;
  • effective mandibular length — Co–Gn.

Their difference is the maxillomandibular differential. The lengths themselves depend on the size of the person, so McNamara gives no single norm for them; instead he links them in a table: each midfacial length corresponds to an expected mandibular length and lower anterior facial height (ANS–Me) [1]. If the mandible is shorter than the table predicts for a given Co–A, this points to mandibular deficiency; if longer, to excess. The conclusion is drawn together with the vertical dimension, because rotation of the mandible changes both the lengths and the chin position.

For the maxillomandibular differential the author gives guide values by face size: small — about 20 mm (20–23 mm in the text), medium — 26–29 mm in the text and 25–27 mm in the step-by-step section, large — 30–33 mm [1].

3. Vertical dimension

Lower anterior facial height (ANS–Me) is assessed together with the jaw lengths, using the same table [1]. The author also gives guide values by face size: small — 60–62 mm; medium — 66–68 mm in the text and 65–67 mm in the step-by-step section; large — 70–74 mm in the text and 70–73 mm in the step-by-step section [1].

The mandibular plane angle is measured between the Frankfort horizontal and Go–Me; according to McNamara it decreases slightly with growth [1]. For the Ann Arbor adults, Afifi et al. report 21.3° ± 3.9° in men and 22.7° ± 4.3° in women [3].

The facial axis angle is the angle between the Ba–N line and the line from PTM to constructed gnathion. McNamara records it as the deviation from 90°: the norm is 0°, negative values indicate excess vertical development and positive values deficient vertical development [1].

4. Mandible to cranial base

Distance from pogonion to the nasion perpendicular [1]:

  • mixed dentition: −8 to −6 mm;
  • adult women: −4 to 0 mm;
  • adult men: −2 to +2 mm.

In other words, the chin normally moves forward relative to the nasion perpendicular with age, and the same value means different things in a child and in an adult.

5. Dentition

Upper incisor to the point A vertical — the distance from the facial surface of the incisor to the line: 4–6 mm regardless of age [1].

Lower incisor to the A–Pog line — 1–3 mm [1].

Airway

McNamara also included an assessment of the pharynx [1]. The upper pharynx is the smallest distance from the posterior outline of the soft palate to the posterior pharyngeal wall; the mean for Ann Arbor adults is 17.4 mm, and the author regards values of about 5 mm or less as a possible sign of airway impairment. The lower pharynx averages 10–12 mm; according to McNamara, values over 15 mm may suggest an anterior tongue position [1]. A lateral ceph only gives a two-dimensional picture, so these figures are a reason for further examination, not a diagnosis.

Norms depend on group and age

McNamara's norms come mainly from North American Caucasian samples, and studies of other populations show clear differences. In Hong Kong, in 12-year-old Chinese schoolchildren (200 boys and 205 girls), Pog to the nasion perpendicular averaged −7.45 mm in boys and −4.88 mm in girls, the upper incisor to the point A vertical 7.34 and 7.86 mm, and the lower incisor to A–Pog 6.35 and 6.26 mm; most measurements differed significantly both between the sexes and from Caucasian peers. The authors recommend separate Chinese norms for boys and girls [2].

In Saudi adults (30 men and 30 women aged 20–25), the jaws were more retrusive, the growth pattern more vertical and the incisors more protrusive than in McNamara's sample: for example, the mandibular plane angle was 31.5° in men and 27.75° in women. Jaw lengths and their difference, however, did not differ significantly. The authors also recommend sex-specific norms and note that mean values are a diagnostic guide, not an individual treatment goal [3].

Age matters just as much: the analysis itself gives different norms for point A and pogonion in the mixed dentition and in adults [1], and the jaw lengths grow with the patient — which is why McNamara compares them with each other rather than with a single figure.

How to read the result

A convenient order is the author's own. First the maxilla (A to the nasion perpendicular), then the ratio of jaw lengths from the table, then the vertical dimension (ANS–Me, mandibular plane angle, facial axis), then the chin position, and only then the incisors and airway. The vertical must always be read together with the sagittal: in a vertical growth pattern the mandible rotates backwards, and the chin ends up behind the nasion perpendicular even with a normal Co–Gn.

Pay special attention to the landmarks. Condylion is often obscured by other structures on a lateral ceph, and all of McNamara's linear measurements are meaningful only on a calibrated radiograph with known magnification. Like any cephalometric analysis, McNamara complements the clinical examination and study models rather than replacing them.
