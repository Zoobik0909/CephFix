Jarabak-Björk cephalometric analysis: growth type and FHI

November 18, 2026 · 10 min read

The Jarabak-Björk analysis (also written Björk-Jarabak) is a lateral cephalometric method focused primarily on the vertical relationships of the facial skeleton and on the direction of mandibular growth. Unlike analyses built on the Frankfort horizontal, it uses the cranial base as its main reference: the S-N line and the posterior cranial base S-Ar. The method describes the growth type (vertical, neutral, horizontal) through the sum of the three angles of the so-called Björk polygon and through the ratio of posterior to anterior face height — the Jarabak ratio, or facial height index (FHI).

History of the method

The foundations were laid by the Danish orthodontist Arne Björk, who studied facial growth on longitudinal material using metallic implants. In "Prediction of mandibular growth rotation" (Am J Orthod, 1969;55:585–599) he described rotation of the mandible during growth and proposed structural signs that may indicate its direction. Later literature lists these signs as: inclination of the condylar head, curvature of the mandibular canal, shape of the lower border of the mandible, inclination of the symphysis, the interincisal angle, the interpremolar or intermolar angle, and anterior lower face height.

Building on Björk's work, Joseph R. Jarabak developed a practical cephalometric analysis presented in J. R. Jarabak and J. A. Fizzell, "Technique and treatment with light-wire edgewise appliances" (St. Louis: Mosby, 1972). As Rodriguez-Cardenas et al. (2014) put it, the Jarabak analysis "mainly considers vertical intermaxillary relationships and uses the cranial base as reference". The ratio of posterior to anterior face height was later used in epidemiological research as well, notably in the study by Siriwat and Jarabak (Angle Orthod, 1985) on the relationship between Angle class of malocclusion and vertical facial morphology.

Landmarks

  • S (Sella) — the centre of the sella turcica; vertex of the saddle angle and the upper point of posterior face height.
  • N (Nasion) — the most anterior point of the frontonasal suture; anterior end of S-N and the upper point of anterior face height.
  • Ar (Articulare) — the point of intersection of the images of the posterior border of the mandibular ramus and the inferior border of the basilar part of the occipital bone. It is a radiographic rather than an anatomical point: its position depends on the projection and on the position of the mandible at the time of exposure.
  • Go (Gonion) — the Jarabak analysis uses a constructed gonion, related to the tangents to the posterior border of the ramus and to the lower border of the mandibular body.
  • Me (Menton) — the lowest point on the symphyseal outline; the lower point of anterior face height and the anterior end of the mandibular body Go-Me.

Angular measurements and the Björk polygon

The line N-S-Ar-Go-Me forms a polygon whose internal angles describe how the cranial base, the ramus and the mandibular body are positioned relative to one another.

  • Saddle angle N-S-Ar — the angle between the anterior and posterior cranial base.
  • Articular angle S-Ar-Go — the angle between the posterior cranial base and the posterior border of the ramus.
  • Gonial angle Ar-Go-Me — the angle between the posterior border of the ramus and the lower border of the mandibular body. The Go-N line divides it into the upper gonial angle Ar-Go-N (reflecting ramus inclination) and the lower gonial angle N-Go-Me (reflecting inclination of the mandibular body).
  • Sum of angles (Björk sum) — N-S-Ar + S-Ar-Go + Ar-Go-Me. According to Rodriguez-Cardenas et al., 396° ±6° corresponds to neutral growth; deviations from this value indicate a hyper- or hypodivergent tendency. A sum above the norm is read as a vertical pattern with clockwise (backward) mandibular rotation; a sum below the norm as a horizontal pattern with counterclockwise (forward) rotation.

Cut-off values for the sum vary between publications. Aguilar-Perez et al. (2024) used: below 390° brachyfacial, 390–402° mesofacial, above 402° dolichofacial. A 2022 paper in The Angle Orthodontist phrases it differently: with a sum greater than 400° the mandible tends towards backward growth rotation, and with a sum less than 392° towards forward rotation.

Linear measurements

  • S-N — length of the anterior cranial base.
  • S-Ar — length of the posterior cranial base.
  • Ar-Go — ramus height.
  • Go-Me — length of the mandibular body.
  • S-Go — posterior face height.
  • N-Me — anterior face height.

FHI (the Jarabak ratio)

The Jarabak facial height index is the ratio of posterior to anterior face height:

FHI = S-Go / N-Me × 100%

The index summarises the vertical component of growth: a relatively short posterior face height (short ramus, clockwise mandibular rotation) lowers the value, a relatively long one raises it. Two main interpretation schemes are used in the literature:

  • below 59% — vertical (clockwise) growth, 59–63% — neutral, above 63% — horizontal (counterclockwise). This scheme is used, for example, by Mahajan et al. (2022, citing Siriwat and Jarabak, 1985) and Aguilar-Perez et al. (2024);
  • 59% or less — hyperdivergent, 60–64% — neutral, 65% or more — hypodivergent (Rodriguez-Cardenas et al., 2014).

In addition, several papers give a reference mean of 63.5% ±1.5%, i.e. a range of 62–65% (Khan et al., 2013; Alshahrani et al., 2018). The difference between schemes matters: a value of 63–64% is horizontal in one scheme and neutral in the other, so a report should state which set of cut-offs was applied.

Clinical interpretation

The analysis is most informative when the values are read together. An increased sum of angles, a reduced FHI, a large lower gonial angle and a short ramus together are consistent with a vertical growth pattern. A reduced sum, a high FHI, a short anterior face height and a small lower gonial angle are typical of a horizontal pattern. The saddle and articular angles help to tell whether the chin position reflects cranial base configuration or the shape of the mandible itself: the same sum may result from an enlarged saddle angle with a normal gonial angle, or from an enlarged gonial angle with a normal cranial base, and the clinical meaning of these variants differs.

Splitting the gonial angle into upper and lower parts helps assess the contribution of the ramus and the mandibular body. A large lower gonial angle reflects the inclination of the mandibular body and is associated with vertical growth; the upper angle relates more to ramus inclination.

Limitations

  • Norms come from specific samples. Studies of Pakistani (Khan et al., 2013) and Saudi (Alshahrani et al., 2018) adults found statistically significant differences from the original norms. For a patient of another ethnic group, or for a child or adolescent, the original adult norms should be applied with caution.
  • Linear values depend on radiographic magnification, sex and age. Absolute dimensions (S-Go, N-Me, Ar-Go) cannot be compared with norms without correcting for magnification; ratios and angles are independent of it.
  • Construction of gonion. Gonion is among the landmarks with the largest identification error: a 2001 paper in The Angle Orthodontist, citing Baumrind and Frantz, reports an estimation error of 3.48 ±1.12 mm for Go versus 1.06 ±0.36 mm for pogonion. Different ways of locating Go (tangent intersection, angle bisector, outline) change the gonial angles and S-Go.
  • The position of Ar depends on whether the teeth are in occlusion during exposure and on overlapping outlines; in the same paper ramus height measured from Ar showed only moderate agreement between techniques.
  • Predictive value. Björk's structural signs are not a reliable stand-alone prediction tool: the authors of a 2005 study in The Angle Orthodontist concluded that morphological growth prediction from structural signs "has to be regarded with skepticism"; hypodivergent subjects were identified much more easily than hyperdivergent ones.

Typical errors

  • Using anatomical gonion instead of the constructed point, which shifts Ar-Go-Me and S-Go.
  • Placing Ar on an open-mouth film, or ignoring double ramus outlines, which shifts the ramus line.
  • Confusing menton and gnathion: in the Jarabak analysis anterior face height and the mandibular body are measured to Me.
  • Interpreting FHI without stating which cut-off scheme is used (59–63% or 60–64%), or treating the 62–65% reference range as the boundaries of neutral growth.
  • Deciding on the growth type from a single value. The sum of angles and FHI reflect different aspects of vertical morphology and may disagree.
  • Comparing a child's absolute linear dimensions with adult norms.
