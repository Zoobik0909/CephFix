Ricketts Cephalometric Analysis: Landmarks, Measurements and Norms

November 9, 2026 · 8 min read

The Ricketts analysis is one of the most detailed cephalometric methods. Unlike the Steiner analysis, which is built on the S–N line, Ricketts takes his measurements from the Frankfort horizontal, the Ba–N line and landmarks of his own such as Xi and Pt. It assesses not only the position of the jaws and incisors but also the facial pattern. This article covers where the analysis came from, which landmarks it needs, what each measurement means, which norms the sources give and how to read them.

Origins

Robert M. Ricketts (1920–2003) was an American orthodontist who trained under Allan G. Brodie at the University of Illinois and taught at Loma Linda University, the University of Illinois at Chicago and the University of Southern California [1]. One of his key cephalometric papers is “A foundation for cephalometric communication” (American Journal of Orthodontics, 1960) [1]. With Carl F. Gugino and Ruel Bench he developed the Bioprogressive philosophy, and with Gugino a system for forecasting growth and treatment results from cephalograms [1]. He summarised decades of work in “Perspectives in the clinical application of cephalometrics: the first fifty years” (The Angle Orthodontist, 1981) [8].

A defining feature of the analysis is that the clinician compares the patient with norms that take age, sex and race into account [1]. The same figures therefore read differently in a 9-year-old and in an adult.

Landmarks and lines

Besides the familiar landmarks (N, A, B, Pog, Gn, ANS, and porion and orbitale for the Frankfort horizontal), the analysis needs several special reference points:

  • Ba (basion) — the anterior margin of the foramen magnum [9].
  • Xi — the centre of the mandibular ramus: the point halfway along the height and depth of the ramus [9].
  • DC — the centre of the condyle: where the condylar axis (Xi–Cd) crosses the Ba–N plane [9].
  • Pm (suprapogonion) — the point where the curvature of the anterior chin outline changes [9].
  • Pt — a point in the region of the foramen rotundum; the facial axis runs through it [5].
  • E-line (esthetic plane) — the line from the tip of the nose to soft-tissue chin [7].
  • PTV — the pterygoid vertical, from which the position of the upper first molar is measured [5].

From these points the main lines are drawn: the Frankfort horizontal (FH), the Ba–N line, the facial axis Pt–Gn, the facial plane N–Pog, the mandibular plane and the A–Pog line, against which the incisors are assessed.

Skeletal measurements

Facial axis — the angle between the Ba–N line and the Pt–Gn line [5]. Norm: 90° ± 3° [2] (another source gives ± 3.5° [3]).

Facial depth (facial angle) — the angle between the Frankfort horizontal and the facial plane N–Pog [5]; it describes the sagittal position of the chin. Norm: 87° ± 3° [2]; [3] gives 87.8° ± 3.6°.

Mandibular plane angle — the angle between the mandibular plane and FH [5]. Norm: 26° ± 4° [2] (± 4.5° in [3]).

Lower face height — the angle ANS–Xi–Pm [5]. Norm: 47° ± 4° [2].

Mandibular arc — the angle between the Pm–Xi and Xi–DC lines [5]; it reflects the shape of the mandible. Norm: 26° ± 4° [2]. For adult Caucasians [4] gives higher values: 31.8° in men and 29.8° in women.

Convexity — the distance in millimetres from point A to the facial plane N–Pog [5]. Norm: 2 mm ± 2 mm [2][3]. For adult Caucasians [4] gives 0.1 mm in men and 0.9 mm in women.

Maxillary depth — the angle between FH and the N–A line [5]. Norm: 90° ± 3° [2][4].

Cranial deflection — the angle between Ba–N and FH [5]; for adult Caucasians [4] gives 27.3° (± 3°). Porion location for adult Caucasians is −38.6 mm (± 2 mm) [4].

Dental measurements

Lower incisor to A–Pog — the distance from the lower incisor edge to the A–Pog line: norm 1 mm ± 2 mm [2][3]. Lower incisor inclination to the same line: 22° ± 4° [2][3].

Upper incisor to A–Pog — 3.5 mm for adult Caucasians [4].

Interincisal angle — the angle between the long axes of the upper and lower incisors. Sources differ: 130° ± 10° [2], and 131° in men and 130° in women with a deviation of ± 4° [4].

Lower incisor to occlusal plane — 1 mm ± 1.25 mm [2].

Upper first molar to PTV — this norm depends on age: the patient’s age in years plus 3 mm (± 3 mm) [2]. For a 9-year-old the guide is therefore 12 mm [2]. For adult Caucasians [4] gives 21.1 mm in men and 17.1 mm in women.

Soft tissue: the E-line

Lower lip to E-line — norm −2 mm ± 2 mm [2][3]: normally the lower lip lies slightly behind the line from the nose tip to the chin.

Norms depend on age and population

The Ricketts norms come from a North American Caucasian sample [2], and other groups show clearly different means. In 50 men and 50 women from Mexico the lower incisor lay 4.6–4.7 mm ahead of A–Pog (versus 1 mm), the upper incisor 8.1–8.7 mm (versus 3.5 mm), and the interincisal angle was 122–123° [4]. In adult Marathas (India) the lower incisor to A–Pog was 4.1 mm in men and 5.5 mm in women, and the facial axis in men was 93.7° [3]. In 48 Iraqi children aged 8–10 facial depth was 81.2°, the mandibular plane angle 30° and convexity 2.7 mm [6].

Age matters just as much. The molar-to-PTV norm depends on age by definition [2], and convexity and the mandibular arc in adults differ from the values given for children [2][4]. A patient should therefore be compared with norms for their own age and group rather than with a single table.

How to read the result

It helps to move from the general to the specific. Start with the vertical: the facial axis, the mandibular plane angle and lower face height are best read together. Then the sagittal: convexity, facial depth and maxillary depth. Next, the incisors relative to A–Pog: are they compensating for a skeletal discrepancy? Finally, the profile against the E-line. Like any cephalometric analysis, Ricketts complements clinical examination and study models rather than replacing them.

Pay special attention to landmarks that are hard to locate. Basion is difficult to identify on a radiograph and carries a large placement error [5], and Xi is constructed from the ramus outlines, so its position depends on how clearly the ramus is visible. Linear measurements (convexity, incisor and molar positions) only mean something on a calibrated image.
