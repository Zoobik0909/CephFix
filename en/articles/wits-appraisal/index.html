The Wits appraisal (Jacobson): construction, norms and limitations

November 27, 2026 · 9 min read

The Wits appraisal assesses the anteroposterior relationship between the maxilla and the mandible without relying on the cranial base. Alexander Jacobson introduced it in 1975 in the paper “The ‘Wits’ appraisal of jaw disharmony” (American Journal of Orthodontics). The name is short for the University of the Witwatersrand, the South African university the method is associated with (VolgGMU, 2023). Unlike the ANB angle, Wits is measured in millimetres along the occlusal plane, so it is not affected by the position of nasion or the inclination of the S-N line.

Why another measurement was needed

The most widely used measure of the sagittal jaw relationship is the ANB angle from the Steiner analysis. Its value, however, depends on more than the position of the jaws relative to each other. Using clinical examples, Jacobson showed that ANB changes when the jaws rotate relative to the cranial reference planes and when nasion is positioned differently: a patient with a normal ANB can have a clear jaw discrepancy, and vice versa (Jacobson 1975). In one of his cases, an ANB of 2° went with a Wits reading of −4.5 mm, which he described as “a definite Class III”.

Later authors supported this reasoning: Wits avoids the errors linked to the position of N and S, so it is used to complement ANB rather than replace it (Kumar et al., Dental Press J Orthod).

Construction

  1. Point A is the deepest point on the anterior contour of the maxillary alveolar process between ANS and the central incisor alveolus.
  2. Point B is the deepest point on the anterior contour of the mandibular alveolar process between the incisor alveolus and the chin.
  3. Occlusal plane. Jacobson drew it through the region of maximum cuspal interdigitation; the recommended reference is the functional occlusal plane, a line through the cusp overlap of the first molars and first premolars (Jacobson 1975; Zawawi 2012). The incisors are not used.
  4. Perpendiculars are dropped from points A and B onto the occlusal plane. Their feet are labelled AO and BO.
  5. The distance AO–BO is measured along the occlusal plane in millimetres.

Sign. If BO lies behind AO, the reading is positive, which is typical of a skeletal Class II. If BO lies ahead of AO, the reading is negative, which is typical of a Class III (Jacobson 1975; Zawawi 2012).

Jacobson's norms

The norms come from adults with excellent occlusion: 21 men and 25 women (Jacobson 1975; Zawawi 2012).

In short, the norms are usually remembered as 0 mm in women and about 1 mm in men, with BO ahead of AO in men. The sign of the male norm is reported differently in the literature. In his summary table, Zawawi (2012) gives −1.17 ± 1.90 mm, which matches “BO ahead of AO” under the sign convention above. The text of the reprinted Jacobson paper and the study by Silwal et al. (2020) give the same value as 1.17 mm without a minus sign. Kumar et al. give the norm as a range of −1 to +1 mm.

Jacobson did not set fixed cut-offs for Class II and Class III. He interpreted the reading together with other measurements and the clinical picture.

The spread within groups (SD 2–4 mm) is often larger than the difference between the norms themselves. Zawawi found a statistically significant difference between men and women (p < 0.001), while in the Nepali sample the difference between the sexes was small. It is therefore better to compare a patient's value with the norm for their population and age.

In children and adolescents, Maspero et al. (2023) found no systematic age trend: in Italian patients aged 6–17, mean values ranged from −0.61 to 0.90 mm, with no significant difference between the sexes. Note that this was a sample of orthodontic patients, not a normative sample.

Limitations

The occlusal plane is hard to locate

The main weakness of Wits is its dependence on the occlusal plane. The plane is located from the cusps of the posterior teeth, which overlap on the cephalogram, and it is even harder to locate in the mixed dentition and in malocclusion. For this reason the reproducibility of Wits has been questioned (Kumar et al.). When the same radiographs were traced again, readings differed by up to 2 mm (1.2 mm on average); on repeated radiographs, by up to 2.5 mm (1.8 mm on average) (Maspero et al. 2023).

The plane's inclination changes the result

Because A and B are projected onto the occlusal plane, its inclination directly affects the AO–BO distance (Kumar et al.): with the same jaw position, a steeper or flatter plane gives different readings. Wits is therefore assessed together with vertical measurements and the inclination of the occlusal plane.

Functional or bisected occlusal plane

Thayer (1990) compared Wits measured on the functional and on the bisected occlusal plane (through the incisor and molar overlap) in 35 males aged 11–24 with Class II division 1. Both versions correlated significantly with skeletal and dental measurements; the bisected-plane version was more closely related to dental measurements, and the functional-plane version slightly more closely to ANB. The author concluded that either plane can be used. Provencal (2016) found correlations of 0.56 to 0.89 between three versions of Wits, meaning they are related but not interchangeable.

Wits and ANB do not always agree

In the study by Kumar et al. (146 patients), Wits correlated with ANB at 0.831, while agreement in classifying Class I, II and III was moderate (kappa 0.489 for Wits versus 0.802 for ANB). The sensitivity of Wits was 0.857 for Class II and 0.902 for Class III. In practice, when ANB and Wits disagree, it is a reason to look closely at the position of nasion and the inclination of S-N and of the occlusal plane rather than to pick one measurement.

An alternative: App-BPP

To get around the instability of the occlusal plane, Nanda and Merrill (1994) proposed projecting points A and B onto the palatal plane (ANS–PNS), whose inclination changes little from age 6 to adulthood. The measurement is called App-BPP. Their norms (as cited by Silwal et al. 2020) are 5.2 ± 2.9 mm in men and 4.8 ± 3.6 mm in women; in the Nepali sample the mean was 3.25 ± 1.20 mm.

How to read the result

  • Compare the value with the norm for the patient's sex: about 0 mm in women and about 1 mm with BO ahead of AO in men (Jacobson 1975), and, where possible, with the norm for the patient's population.
  • Positive values (BO behind AO) point towards a skeletal Class II tendency, negative values towards Class III.
  • Keep accuracy in mind: a difference of 1–2 mm can be within tracing error.
  • Assess Wits together with ANB, the occlusal plane inclination and vertical measurements.
