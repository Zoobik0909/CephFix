Steiner cephalometric analysis: landmarks, measurements and norms

November 7, 2026 · 6 min read

The Steiner analysis is one of the most widely used cephalometric methods. It is often the first one students learn: it has few measurements, they fit together logically, and the result immediately shows how the jaws and teeth relate. This article covers where the analysis came from, which landmarks it needs, what each measurement means, which norms the sources give, and where the analysis can mislead.

Origins

American orthodontist Cecil C. Steiner described his method in “Cephalometrics for You and Me” (1953), then developed it in “Cephalometrics in Clinical Practice” (1959) and “Use of Cephalometrics as an Aid to Planning and Assessing Orthodontic Treatment” (1960) [1]. The analysis has three parts: skeletal (the jaws relative to the skull and to each other), dental (the incisors relative to the jaws and to each other) and soft tissue (the lower facial profile) [1].

Steiner’s key idea was to use the S–N line (sella–nasion), which represents the anterior cranial base, as the reference. He chose it over the Frankfort horizontal because orbitale and porion are hard to locate reliably on a radiograph [2].

Landmarks

The skeletal and dental parts use these landmarks (definitions after [2]):

  • S (sella) — the midpoint of the sella turcica.
  • N (nasion) — the most anterior point of the frontonasal suture.
  • A (subspinale) — the deepest point of the anterior maxillary concavity.
  • B (supramentale) — the deepest point of the anterior concavity of the mandibular symphysis.
  • Pog (pogonion) — the most anterior point of the symphysis.
  • Gn (gnathion) — a point on the symphysis outline between pogonion and menton.
  • Go (gonion) — the most posterior inferior point on the angle of the mandible.
  • Me (menton) — the lowest point of the symphysis.
  • Incisal edges and root apices of the upper and lower incisors — for the tooth axes.

From these points the lines are drawn: S–N, N–A, N–B, the mandibular plane Go–Gn and the occlusal plane. The soft-tissue part uses the S-line, which joins soft-tissue pogonion to the middle of the S-shaped curve of the lower border of the nose [2].

Skeletal measurements

SNA — the angle between S–N and N–A. It shows whether the maxilla is normally positioned, prognathic or retrognathic. Norm: about 82° ± 2° [2].

SNB — the angle between S–N and N–B; the same for the mandible. Norm: about 80° ± 2° [2].

ANB — the difference between SNA and SNB, the sagittal jaw relationship. Norm: about 2° ± 2°. An ANB of 4° or more suggests skeletal Class II; 0° or less suggests Class III [2].

SND — the angle between S–N and N–D, where D is the centre of the mandibular symphysis. Norm: 76° [3].

GoGn–SN — the angle of the mandibular plane to S–N. Norm: 32° [1][3]. It is used to judge the growth pattern: a large angle suggests vertical growth, a small one horizontal growth [1].

Occlusal plane to SN — norm 14° [1][3].

S–L and S–E — distances along SN from S to the projections of pogonion (L) and of the posterior border of the condyle (E). They describe the sagittal position of the mandible. Norms: 51 mm and 22 mm [3].

Dental measurements

U1–NA — the inclination and position of the upper incisor relative to N–A: the angle of the tooth axis to the line and the distance from the line to the labial surface of the incisor. Norm: 22° and 4 mm [1][3].

L1–NB — the same for the lower incisor relative to N–B. Norm: 25° and 4 mm [1][3].

Interincisal angle (U1–L1) — the angle between the long axes of the upper and lower incisors. Norm: about 130°; tables give 130° [1][4] or 131° [3][5].

Pog–NB — the prominence of the chin ahead of N–B. Steiner assessed it together with the distance from the lower incisor to N–B [1]; sources give different stand-alone norms for Pog–NB.

Soft tissue: the S-line

Ideally both lips touch the S-line [2]; the table in [3] records the norm as 0 mm. Lips ahead of the line are considered protrusive [1].

Norms are not dogma: ethnicity and age

Steiner’s norms came from a Caucasian sample and may differ for other groups. For example, in 76 Palestinian students with normal profiles the skeletal angles almost matched Steiner (SNA 81.9°, SNB 79.2°, GoGn–SN 31.7°). The incisors, however, were clearly more protrusive: U1–NA 6.9 mm and L1–NB 7.1 mm versus 4 mm; the interincisal angle was 126.6° and the occlusal plane 17° [5]. In other words, what Steiner’s table calls protrusion may be normal for a given population. That is why a patient should be compared with norms for their own group and age.

Where the analysis can mislead

The weak spot is nasion. It is unstable during growth: a posterior nasion increases ANB, an anterior nasion decreases it [2]. A short or steep S–N line and jaw rotation also distort the values [2]. So it is wise to cross-check ANB with other sagittal indicators, such as the Wits appraisal or APDI.

The second common error is placing points A and B. They are the “deepest” points of a contour, and their position depends on image quality and head posture. A 1–2 mm error changes SNA, SNB, ANB and the incisor positions relative to NA and NB at once. The third is linear measurement without calibration: millimetres on a digital image only mean something if the scale is known.

How to read the result

Start with the skeleton: ANB gives the class, SNA and SNB show which jaw is responsible. GoGn–SN adds the vertical dimension. Then the teeth: are the incisors compensating for the skeletal discrepancy with their inclination? Finally, the profile against the S-line. The Steiner analysis does not replace clinical examination and study models; it is one diagnostic tool among several.
