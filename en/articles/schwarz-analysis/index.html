Schwarz cephalometric analysis: angles I, F, H, B, jaw proportions and facial types

November 25, 2026 · 9 min read

The Schwarz analysis is one of the oldest European systems for evaluating the lateral cephalogram. Its author, the Viennese orthodontist Artur Martin Schwarz, worked on cephalometry from 1931 and summarised the method in his book "Die Röntgenostatik" (1958); an English account, "Roentgenostatics", appeared in the American Journal of Orthodontics in 1961. The method is still part of orthodontic teaching materials in Russia, Belarus and Ukraine and of German-language dissertations, so it is worth knowing which lines it is built on, what its norms are and how later studies relate to those norms.

The idea: craniometry and gnathometry

As early as 1935, Schwarz proposed separating two tasks: craniometry — the position of the jaws relative to the cranial base — and gnathometry — the relationship of the maxilla and mandible to each other (Loserth 2008; Baier 2007). The analysis therefore has two groups of measurements: angles describing the inclination and position of the jaws relative to the anterior cranial base, and proportions that compare jaw lengths with the anterior cranial base length of the individual patient.

Landmarks and lines

  • Se — the midpoint of the entrance to the sella turcica. Schwarz used it instead of the sella centre S (Loserth 2008; Shkarin et al. 2021).
  • N-Se — the anterior cranial base line.
  • FH — in the Schwarz analysis the Frankfort horizontal on the cephalogram runs from condylion to orbitale; a variant from the upper margin of the external acoustic meatus to orbitale is used when condylion is not clearly visible (Loserth 2008).
  • Pn — the nasion perpendicular: on the cephalogram, the perpendicular to N-Se through soft-tissue nasion (Loserth 2008). Po — the perpendicular to N-Se through orbitale. Schwarz called the space between Pn and Po the jaw profile field.
  • SpP — the spinal (palatal) plane, ANS–PNS.
  • MT1 — the tangent to the lower border of the mandibular body; MT2 — the tangent to the posterior border of the ramus.
  • N-A — the "forehead–jaw line" (Stirn-Kiefer-Linie).

Note two differences from the familiar American analyses: the sella reference point is Se rather than S, and the Frankfort horizontal passes through condylion rather than porion. If Schwarz norms are applied to tracings built on other points, the values may shift.

Angular measurements and norms

The mean values Schwarz regarded as norms are given in Loserth's dissertation (2008) and in Smeets' paper (1964), which discusses the method in detail. These works give no "±" ranges for angles I, F, H and G; ranges appear in the teaching materials of the Department of Orthodontics at Poltava State Medical University (PSMU).

The norms are consistent with each other: with an inclination angle of 85° and a basal angle of 20°, the angle between Pn and the mandibular plane is exactly 65°.

Reading the angles

  • Angle I describes the inclination of the maxilla. Above the norm indicates anteinclination (the spinal plane tilted upward anteriorly); below the norm indicates retroinclination (Smeets 1964; Shkarin et al. 2021).
  • Angle F describes the position of the maxilla and face relative to the cranial base. Above the norm indicates anteposition; below the norm, retroposition (Shkarin et al. 2021; PSMU materials).
  • Angle B reflects the vertical relationship of the jaws: the larger it is, the more the spinal and mandibular planes diverge. PSMU materials associate a larger B angle with shorter lateral and longer anterior segments.
  • Angle H is interpreted in PSMU materials through the position of the condyles: a smaller angle indicates supraposition, a larger one infraposition.

The individual norm

An important feature of the method is that Schwarz did not compare the facial angle with the mean value alone. According to Smeets (1964), the individual norm for angle F is the same patient's inclination angle I: if F is larger than I, the face is anteposed; if smaller, retroposed. In the example Smeets cites, with I = 75° and F = 83°, the face is judged retroinclined and anteposed, even though F is close to the mean norm of 85°.

Jaw proportions

The linear part of the analysis is not based on absolute norms in millimetres but on proportions to the patient's own anterior cranial base length N-Se (Loserth 2008; Shkarin et al. 2021):

By comparing actual and target dimensions, the clinician judges whether each jaw is short or long relative to the cranial base and to the other jaw.

Sources differ on age. The formulas given by Loserth contain no age correction. A Volgograd State Medical University textbook, citing Kolotkov, states that the mandibular body is 1–2 mm longer than N-Se in the mixed dentition and 5–7 mm longer in the permanent dentition (Shkarin et al. 2021). The "N-Se + 3 mm" rule should therefore not be applied mechanically in children.

Schwarz facial types

Schwarz distinguished three basic facial types by the position of the profile relative to the nasion perpendicular Pn: the average face (Durchschnittsgesicht), the anterior face (Vorgesicht) and the posterior face (Rückgesicht). Each can be straight or slanted forward or backward, giving nine variants (Loserth 2008; Baier 2007). According to Loserth, the type is first assessed on a profile photograph and finally on the cephalogram. The straight anterior and straight posterior faces run parallel to the average face and, according to Schwarz, are aesthetically equivalent to it.

Smeets (1964) links the types to the angles: the slant of the profile depends on whether the inclination angle I is larger or smaller than the mean 85°, and the position of a straight face on how angle F relates to angle I. In the Russian school, the nine Schwarz types were adapted by F. Ya. Khoroshilkina and co-authors on the basis of angles F and I, measured from the sella centre S rather than Se (Nabatchikova et al. 2013).

What later studies show

A test of the analysis on a Munich sample (61 children and 32 adults with harmonious faces) found mean values close to, but not identical with, Schwarz's norms: angle J 87.3° in children and 86.4° in adults, angle F 84.8° and 86.5°, basal angle B 26.5° and 22.1°. The mandibular body-to-ramus ratio was 7 : 5.31 in children and 7 : 5.87 in adults. The author concluded that the target proportions depend on age and found no correlation between actual and target proportions (Loserth 2008).

Smeets (1964), studying 100 children aged 7–12 with Class II division 1 malocclusion, considered Schwarz's hypotheses broadly confirmed; the correlation between angle F and the SNPg angle was 0.628. In a Russian study of 58 people with physiological occlusion (Verstakov et al. 2024), the mean A-N-Se angle in normoposition was 85.04°, and the authors propose 83–87° as the normal range.

The practical conclusion: Schwarz norms are a convenient reference, but in children and in other populations the means shift, so results are best interpreted together with other analyses and with the patient's age in mind.
