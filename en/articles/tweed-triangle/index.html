The Tweed Triangle: FMA, IMPA and FMIA — Construction, Norms and Treatment Goals

November 20, 2026 · 10 min read

The Tweed triangle (Tweed's diagnostic facial triangle) is one of the simplest cephalometric analyses and one of the most practical. It consists of only three angles, yet it addresses a question an orthodontist asks when planning almost every treatment: where should the lower incisors be positioned for a given vertical facial pattern? This article covers the history of the method, how the triangle is constructed, Tweed's original norms and his treatment goals depending on the FMA, population data and the limitations of the analysis.

History

Charles H. Tweed described the role of the angle between the Frankfort horizontal and the mandibular plane in "The Frankfort-mandibular plane angle in orthodontic diagnosis, classification, treatment planning, and prognosis" (Am J Orthod Oral Surg, 1946). In 1954 the Angle Orthodontist published his paper "The Frankfort-mandibular incisor angle (FMIA) in orthodontic diagnosis, treatment planning and prognosis" (1954;24(3):121–169), in which the FMIA took centre stage. Tweed later returned to the rationale of the triangle in papers in the American Journal of Orthodontics in 1962 and 1969.

In an interview with the Journal of Clinical Orthodontics (1968), Tweed stressed that the FMIA remains relatively stable during growth, while the FMA and IMPA fluctuate. This is why his treatment target is the position of the lower incisor relative to the Frankfort horizontal.

How the triangle is constructed

The triangle is formed by three lines: the Frankfort horizontal, the mandibular plane and the long axis of the lower central incisor. Where they intersect they form three angles — FMA, IMPA and FMIA (Jain et al., 2017). Because these are the angles of one triangle, they add up to 180°, as Tweed's own classic example shows: 25° + 90° + 65°.

  • FMA (Frankfort mandibular plane angle) — the angle between the Frankfort horizontal and the mandibular plane. It describes the vertical facial pattern.
  • IMPA (incisor mandibular plane angle) — the angle between the long axis of the lower central incisor and the mandibular plane. It shows the inclination of the incisor relative to its basal bone.
  • FMIA (Frankfort mandibular incisor angle) — the angle between the Frankfort horizontal and the long axis of the lower incisor.

The Charles H. Tweed International Foundation, in its case report presentation requirements, describes the construction of the lines as follows:

  • Frankfort horizontal: a line connecting a point 4.5 mm above the geometric centre of the ear rod with an orbital point located midway between the lower borders of the left and right orbits. In other words, the Foundation uses an ear-rod (machine porion) construction rather than anatomic porion.
  • Mandibular plane: anteriorly it touches menton, and posteriorly it bisects the distance between the right and left lower borders of the mandible in the region of the gonial angle — essentially a tangent to the lower border, averaging the two sides.
  • Incisor axis: a line along the long axis of the lower central incisor, extended down to the mandibular plane and up to the Frankfort horizontal.

The definition of the mandibular plane varies in the literature. A tangent to the lower border is used by the Tweed Foundation, by the review of Jain et al. (2017) and by several population studies; other studies draw the plane through Go and Me (for example, Ajisafe et al., 2020; Hernández-Sayago et al., 2013). The construction affects FMA and IMPA, so when comparing a result with norms it matters how the plane was drawn.

Tweed's norms

The norms FMA 25°, IMPA 90° and FMIA 65° became established in the literature; as Rizvi and Hossain (2017) note, they were widely accepted and followed. Tweed also reported mean values measured on the radiographs of his sample, which differ somewhat from the rounded "visual" values.

Treatment goals depending on FMA

Tweed's key clinical idea is that the target inclination of the lower incisors depends on the vertical pattern. In the JCO interview (1968) he put it as follows:

Patient's FMATweed's treatment goal (JCO, 1968)
20° to 30°attain an FMIA of 68°
30° and aboveattain an FMIA of 65°
below 20° (flat angle)IMPA not greater than 94°

In the same interview Tweed notes that as the FMA decreases from 20° to about 12°, the FMIA readings rise from 68° to 75° or more.

Secondary sources restate this scheme in different ways. Jain et al. (2017) and Derwich et al. (2021) give an FMA range of 21–29° for the FMIA 68° goal, and give different IMPA limits for low-angle cases: 90° or 94° (Jain et al.) and 92° (Derwich et al.). Ajisafe et al. (2020) give an FMA range of 22–28° for an FMIA of 68°. As a general guide, Derwich et al. cite an FMIA of 65–68° and an IMPA of 90 ± 5°. It is therefore helpful to state in a report which scheme the assessment is based on.

Population differences

Tweed's norms come from a sample of European descent — this is how Kumari and Nayan (2019) label them. Studies of other groups with harmonious profiles or normal occlusion show a similar FMA but markedly greater lower incisor inclination (IMPA) and, accordingly, a smaller FMIA.

In Goldsman's sample the IMPA was measured against a different reference line, so it is not shown in the table. These data do not mean that incisors in patients from these groups "should" be more proclined; they show that Tweed's norms cannot simply be transferred to any population.

Limitations

  • Reliability of the Frankfort horizontal. McNamara (1984) recommended using anatomic rather than machine porion and noted that machine porion can be as much as 1 cm away from anatomic porion. Pancherz and Gökbuget (1996) found that anatomic porion had the larger identification error, while machine porion lay about 9 mm lower and about 2 mm more anterior and was judged unsuitable for constructing the Frankfort horizontal.
  • Landmark identification error. In the study by Chen et al. (2000), differences between observers were generally under 1 mm, with the exception of Or, Me, PNS and the lower incisor apex; on the original films the inter-observer error was 1.46 mm for Or, 1.73 mm for Go and 1.75 mm for the lower incisor apex. All of these points are used to construct the triangle.
  • Reproducibility of the FMIA. Comparing measurements from three radiology clinics on 40 radiographs, Dias da Silveira and Dias Silveira (2006) found differences in the FMIA of up to 20.13° and concluded that the measurements were not reproducible.
  • Different definitions of the mandibular plane (tangent or Go-Me) make it harder to compare results across studies.

The Tweed triangle and Merrifield's analysis

Levern L. Merrifield extended Tweed's approach with an assessment of the soft-tissue profile. The profile line is drawn from soft-tissue chin tangent to the most prominent lip, and the Z-angle is formed by the intersection of this line with the Frankfort horizontal (Tweed Foundation). According to Merrifield (1966), as cited by Al-Zubaidi (2009), the Z-angle norm is 78 ± 5° at ages 11–15 and 80 ± 5° in adults; a later paper by Merrifield (1996) gives 75–78° as the recommended value (Derwich et al., 2021). Merrifield and co-authors later described a differential diagnostic analysis system (Am J Orthod Dentofacial Orthop, 1994), but a detailed account of it is beyond the scope of this article.

Common mistakes

  • Comparing a result with a norm obtained with a different construction of the mandibular plane or Frankfort horizontal.
  • Drawing the incisor axis along the labial surface of the crown instead of along the long axis of the tooth.
  • Assessing IMPA without regard to FMA: the same incisor inclination may be acceptable in one vertical pattern and not in another.
  • Applying Tweed's norms to patients from other populations without considering population data.
